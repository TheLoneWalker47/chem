01 · Electrolysis · Charles

Electrolysis

Electrolysis uses an external source of electrical energy to drive a non-spontaneous chemical reaction in an electrolyte.

Electrolyte

A molten ionic compound or solution containing mobile ions.

Cathode (−)

Reduction occurs: positive ions gain electrons.

Anode (+)

Oxidation occurs: negative ions lose electrons.

Applications

Electroplating, metal extraction and purification of copper.

In an electrolytic cell, the cathode is negative and the anode is positive.

02 · Faraday’s Laws · Pritam

Faraday’s Laws

Faraday’s laws describe how the amount of substance formed at an electrode depends on the electric charge passed.

First law

The mass deposited or liberated is directly proportional to the total charge passed: m ∝ Q.

Second law

For the same charge, the masses of different substances are proportional to their chemical equivalent masses.

Combined equation

m = (M × I × t) / (n × F)

Symbols

M = molar mass; I = current; t = time; n = electrons transferred; F ≈ 96,485 C mol⁻¹.

Since Q = I × t, increasing current or time increases the charge passed.

03 · Movement of charge · Ankon

Electrical conductance

An electrolyte solution conducts electricity because dissolved ions are free to move and carry charge.

Cations (+)

Positive ions move toward the cathode.

Anions (−)

Negative ions move toward the anode.

Conductance, G

How easily a conductor carries current. G = 1/R; unit: siemens (S).

Conductivity, κ

Conducting ability accounting for the cell geometry. Unit: S m⁻¹.

Both positive and negative ions contribute to the flow of charge through an electrolyte.

Oppositely charged ions migrate in opposite directions
04 · A working example · Tashfiah

Cells, half-reactions & the Daniell cell

A Daniell cell converts a spontaneous zinc–copper redox reaction into electrical energy.

Anode · oxidation
Zn(s) → Zn²⁺(aq) + 2e⁻

Zinc releases electrons.

Cathode · reduction
Cu²⁺(aq) + 2e⁻ → Cu(s)

Copper ions gain electrons.

Zn + Cu²⁺ → Zn²⁺ + Cu

The salt bridge completes the ionic circuit and helps maintain charge balance. Electrons travel through the wire from zinc to copper.

05 · Measuring cell potential · Nodhi

EMF & reference cells

Cell EMF is the potential difference between its electrodes when no current is being drawn (open-circuit condition).

1
Measure

Use a high-input-impedance voltmeter to minimize current draw.

2
Compare

A reference electrode has a known, stable potential under specified conditions.

3
Calculate

E°cell = E°cathode − E°anode, using standard reduction potentials.

Example reference: the Standard Hydrogen Electrode (SHE), assigned 0.00 V under standard conditions. Real measurements depend on temperature, concentrations and setup.
